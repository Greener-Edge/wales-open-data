UK food risk: a free open data tool from Greener Edge Sustainability

A third of the UK's food imports come from places short of water. For fruit and nuts, it's two-thirds.

We mapped where the UK's $72 billion a year of food imports come from, and checked every origin for water stress, drought, flooding, warming and deforestation.

In brief

What we found. Most of the risk sits in fruit, vegetables, olive oil and nuts, much of it from Spain and other dry regions. Deforestation risk is smaller, but concentrated in cocoa, beef, soy and palm oil.

Why it matters. The UK grows only a small share of these foods itself, so it relies on other countries' water and weather. That risk is already here, and grows as the climate warms.

Where the risk sits

What this shows: The UK's 70 biggest food imports, sized by spend. The ring shows which of four risks each faces, and a stronger colour means more risks. Select a food group to zoom in, or sort everything by exposure.

Why it matters: A handful of big, everyday foods carry several risks at once. That's where supply problems would be felt first.

A risk is flagged when over half of a food comes from water-stressed countries, over half from drought-risk countries, over half from its single biggest supplier, or when its crop carries more than 500 ha of deforestation risk. Press Escape or select the background to zoom back out. On a phone, swipe the chart sideways to see it all.

The risk is already here

What this shows: The share of each food type the UK imports from water-stressed countries. The bar shows today; the line shows 2050, business as usual. Change the year and scenario in the explorer below.

Why it matters: For most foods the line barely moves, because the main suppliers are already in the highest stress bands: the risk isn't coming, it's already here. On top of that, the places that grow UK food are projected to warm by about 3.2°C by the 2080s under business as usual, against 2.5°C for the UK.

Wales: what we grow

85% of Welsh farmland is grass, and fruit and vegetables use less than 0.1%. Wales farms 1.77 million hectares, mostly feeding sheep and cattle, because that's what the land, soil and rain suit best.

What Wales grows, and what the UK buys in

Welsh farming is built on grass, livestock, milk and eggs. The UK's most climate-exposed imports are the foods that use the least Welsh land: two-thirds of imported fruit and nuts, and 61% of vegetables, come from water-stressed countries. Wales can't grow citrus or cocoa, but the gap raises a fair question about what more could be grown closer to home.

How we worked

We grouped FAOSTAT's 425 products into food types and removed non-food items such as tobacco, cotton, wool, live animals and pet food. "Water-stressed" means Aqueduct's high (40-80%) or extremely high (over 80%) bands. "Drought risk" means its medium-high or high drought bands. Water stress and drought are weighted by irrigated agriculture; river flood risk is weighted by population, as that's the only weighting WRI publishes for it.

Future water stress uses WRI's 2030, 2050 and 2080 projections. Warming uses SSP1-2.6, SSP3-7.0 and SSP5-8.5, shown under the same optimistic, business as usual and pessimistic labels. "Average warming where it's grown" weights each supplier country by the value of food it sends the UK.

Deforestation for processed foods, such as chocolate or soy oil, is shown for the whole crop family, because the deforestation data is measured at crop level. Supplier concentration is the share of UK imports of a food from its single biggest supplier. "Grown in the UK" compares production with domestic supply by weight, averaged over three years.

Limits

Trade figures show the country recorded as the partner, which can be where food was processed or shipped from rather than grown. Belgium and the Netherlands, for example, handle a lot of food grown elsewhere.

Country scores are averages, so a supplier may farm in a less stressed or cooler region. Deforestation figures are national averages that measure risk exposure, not proof that a particular product caused clearing, and count direct imports only.

A home-grown food can still rely on imported feed, fertiliser or energy. Welsh survey estimates are scaled up from the farms that respond (50% in 2025).
