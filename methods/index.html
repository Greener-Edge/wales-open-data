How we built each part of the series: which data we used, what we did to it, what it can and can't tell you, and how it's licensed. Last updated 5 October 2026.

Our principles

  • We only use public, openly licensed data. No client data is used anywhere in the series.
  • Where we derive a figure, such as a grouping or a weighted average, we say so on the page.
  • We describe risk and exposure, not cause. A country's average score doesn't tell you about a particular farm or product.
  • When we find an error, we fix it and record it below.

Where did Wales's emissions go?

Data

Department for Energy Security and Net Zero (DESNZ), UK local authority and regional greenhouse gas emissions statistics, 2005 to 2024, published June 2026 (table 1.1, full dataset). Council boundaries from the Office for National Statistics, via the UK-GeoJSON project.

What we did

  • Extracted the 22 Welsh local authorities, every sector and year.
  • Per person figures divide emissions by mid-year population.
  • Grouped DESNZ sub-sectors into drivers (electricity, landfill, large industrial sites, gas and so on) to show what changed between 2005 and 2024. These groupings are ours.
  • Checked our Wales totals against the DESNZ statistical release (43.8 to 23.5 MtCO₂e, a 46% fall).

Limits

  • These are territorial emissions. Aviation, shipping, military transport and fluorinated gases are excluded.
  • Electricity emissions combine a cleaner grid and lower use; the data doesn't separate them.
  • Land use and forestry can be negative, where land absorbs more carbon than it releases.
  • DESNZ advises caution, as councils have limited influence over some sources.

Is Wales's commercial property ready for EPC B?

Data

Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government, Energy Performance of Buildings Data for England and Wales, non-domestic certificates, downloaded 2 October 2026.

What we did

  • Filtered to the 22 Welsh councils.
  • Kept the most recent certificate per building, matched on property reference (UPRN) where available, otherwise on address.
  • Counted certificates lodged in the last 10 years as current.
  • "Large" means a floor area over 1,000 m². "Last certified for letting" means the latest certificate was issued because the property was being let.
  • Removed all address data before analysis. Only council-level counts are published.

Limits

  • The register only covers buildings that have needed an EPC.
  • It can't tell us which buildings are rented today, so 2031 figures show buildings that would be in scope if rented.
  • The 2031 EPC B standard comes from the UK government's interim response of June 2026 and still needs secondary legislation.

Where does the UK's food come from, and what's at risk?

LayerSourceMeasure we show

What we did

  • Grouped FAOSTAT's products into food types and removed non-food items (tobacco, cotton, wool, live animals, pet food and similar).
  • Removed FAOSTAT's "rice, paddy equivalent" total, which double counts individual rice products.
  • "Water-stressed" means Aqueduct's high or extremely high bands; "drought risk" means its medium-high or high bands.
  • Future scenarios are shown as optimistic (SSP1-2.6), business as usual (SSP3-7.0) and pessimistic (SSP5-8.5).
  • "Average warming where it's grown" weights each supplier country by the value of food it sends the UK.
  • Deforestation for processed foods is shown for the whole crop family, as the source data is at crop level.
  • The supplier shock simulator removes a share of one country's supply and reports the share of each UK import lost, before any switching to other suppliers. It's illustrative.
  • Meal ingredient lists are our illustrative choices, and show only the imported part of each ingredient.

Limits

  • Trade figures use the recorded partner country, which may be where food was processed or shipped from rather than grown. Belgium and the Netherlands handle a lot of food grown elsewhere.
  • Country scores are national averages. A supplier may farm in a less stressed or cooler region.
  • Deforestation estimates measure risk exposure, not proof that a product caused clearing, and count direct imports only.
  • Values are in US dollars, as FAOSTAT reports them.

Check your supply chain

How it works

  • Uses the same datasets as the food imports map. Each ingredient is matched to a FAOSTAT product and scored for its chosen origin country.
  • Where the origin is unknown, it uses the UK's 2024 import mix for that food and reports the share coming from flagged countries.
  • Deforestation is flagged when UK imports of that crop from that country are linked to 10 ha or more of deforestation risk in 2023 (500 ha for the import mix).
  • Results are weighted by spend when spend is entered for every ingredient; otherwise each ingredient counts equally.
  • The supplier questions are prompts based on the flags, not advice.

Privacy

The checker runs entirely in the browser. Nothing entered is sent to Greener Edge or anyone else. Lists are saved in the user's own browser storage so they can return to them.

Limits

  • Country averages can't reflect a specific farm, region or certification.
  • It's a screening tool, not an audit or a footprint.

What does Wales grow?

Data

Welsh Government, Survey of agriculture and horticulture: June 2025, including the historical series from 1867.

What we did

  • Grouped land use into grass and grazing, arable, farm woodland, fruit and vegetables, and other land.
  • Sheep per person uses the 2024 mid-year population of Welsh councils.

Limits

  • Survey estimates are scaled up from responding farms (a 50% response rate in 2025).
  • Council-level farm figures aren't currently published online. We've asked Welsh Government for them.
